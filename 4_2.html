Chapter 2: The Old English

English, a language spoken by over 1.5 billion people worldwide, has a rich history and fascinating origins. Its roots can be traced back to the Germanic languages spoken by the Angles, Saxons, and Jutes, who arrived in England in the 5th century. These tribes brought with them their own distinct dialects, which eventually merged together to form Old English.

It's key to mention that English was heavily influenced by Latin and Greek, considered the languages of learning and scholarship at the time. Latin was particularly important, as it was the language of the Christian Church, which had a significant impact on the development of the language. In addition to this, the Latin alphabet was also adopted for writing Old English, an English which had a much more complex grammar and more inflections than the modern one. Old English also used a different alphabet, with letters such as "thorn" (þ) and "wynn" (ƿ), that are no longer used today.

Vikings played a crucial role in the evolution of English as well. The Viking invasions in the 9th and 10th centuries brought Old Norse into England, which merged with Old English to create Middle English. Middle English was heavily influenced by French, after the Norman Conquest of 1066, which saw the Normans bring their own version of Old French to England. This led to a period of linguistic mixing and borrowing, and Middle English evolved into Early Modern English by the 16th century.

Another remarkable element in the evolution of English has to do with one of the most influential figures in the history of languages and literature. We are talking about William Shakespeare. Shakespeare is credited with adding thousands of new words and phrases to the English language, many of which are still in use today. Some of his contributions include words we use in multiple fields such as "road," "manager," and "suspicious."

Despite the global popularity of English today, it was not always the dominant language. In fact, during the Middle Ages, French was the language of the English nobility and the legal system. It was only after the Hundred Years' War between England and France that English became the primary language of England.

Chapter 3: The Evolution of English

Early Modern English was the language spoken during the 16th, 17th, and 18th centuries and was shaped by many factors, including the expansion of trade and commerce, the growth of the British Empire, and the printing industry. During this time, many new words were added to the English language, particularly from Latin and Greek. This led to the creation of many words that are still used today, such as "television" and "telephone.

One of the most significant changes to the English language during this period was the Great Vowel Shift. This was a series of changes to the pronunciation of English vowels that took place between the 14th and 18th centuries. These changes led to new ways to pronounce words such as “meet”, as in “let’s meet tomorrow”; and “meat”, as in, “I don’t eat meat”. They sound the same: “Meet” and “Meat”, but pronunciation in old English was different. After the Great Vowel Shift, they adopted the same long - E - sound.

Around the same period of time, more specifically in the late 16th century, dictionaries, grammars, and style guides were published, which helped to standardize the English language. This standardization allowed English to become a global language and helped to establish its dominance in science and literature.

One of the most influential figures in the standardization of English was Samuel Johnson. In 1755, Johnson published "A Dictionary of the English Language," which was the first comprehensive English dictionary. It contained over 40,000 words, each with a definition and examples of usage. The dictionary was so popular that it remained the standard English dictionary for over 150 years.

Not long after that, during the Industrial Revolution of the 18th and 19th centuries, English underwent significant changes, with many new words and phrases being added to the language. The Industrial Revolution brought about many new inventions and technologies, such as the steam engine and the telegraph, which required new words to describe them. These new words were created out of known sounds, but they added some slight spelling variations and syllabic formation to add some extra power to the words, given how revolutionary these inventions were.

Now, in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, English became the language of international diplomacy, with the British Empire being the dominant world power. This led to the development of what is known as "International English," which is a simplified form of English used for communication between people who speak different native languages. Today, International English is the most commonly spoken form of English and is used in business, education, and international organizations.

The evolution of English continues to this day, with new words and phrases being added all the time. One of the most significant changes in recent years has been the influence of social media. The Internet has allowed for the rapid spread of new words and phrases, such as "hashtag" and "viral," which have become part of the mainstream lexicon.

Chapter 4: Today’s English

Today, English is used in almost every country in the world and has become the lingua franca of international business, science, and technology. English has also become the language of popular culture, with movies, music, and television shows being produced in English and enjoyed by people all over the world. This has led to concerns about cultural imperialism and the loss of linguistic diversity. The widespread use of English as a second language has also led to the development of different varieties of English, such as Indian English and Nigerian English, which have their own unique vocabulary and grammar.

The truth is that English will continue to evolve; and not only because of the advances in technology, but because it is within the nature of languages. And, as the world becomes more and more interconnected, we will see this evolution taking place more rapidly.

New words and expressions are being added all the time. Many of these words come from other languages, such as French, Spanish, and Arabic. In fact, around 80% of the current English words have roots in other languages and the rise of social media and the Internet will continue to lead us to the creation of many new words. 10 years ago, for example, we didn’t have words such as “selfie” or “hater”, nor phrases, such as “let’s google it” or “whatsapp me”, and this is due to the influence of globalization.

As more and more people around the world use English as a second language, there is a growing demand for simplified versions of the language that are easier to learn. This has led to the development of "Globish," a simplified version of English that is designed to be more accessible to non-native speakers as it uses a subset of standard English grammar and a list of 1500 English words, which is fascinating and practical.

It is clear that English will continue to play a pivotal role in the world for years to come. English has already proven to be a powerful force for communication and global understanding, and it is likely to continue to be so, at least in the near future.

In spite of the dominance of English, there is still a need to preserve linguistic diversity and promote the use of other languages. This is why efforts are being made to promote multilingualism and encourage the use of indigenous languages. It is important to remember that language is an integral part of culture and identity, and the preservation of linguistic diversity is crucial for maintaining a healthy and vibrant world.
