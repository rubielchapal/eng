Curso de Construcción de Oraciones en Inglés

Nouns

Nouns are anything you can name. For example:

  • Persons (Manager)
  • Places (Office)
  • Things (Notebook)
  • Living things (Pet)
  • Activities (Reading)
  • Ideas (Memory)
  • Feelings (Anger)

Nouns are divided into common nouns and proper nouns.

Common Nouns

Common nouns are the general names we give to a person, place, thing, emotion, etc.

Examples:

  • I work in a big company.
  • My coworker said, "hello."
  • Her cell phone fell and broke.

Proper Nouns

These are specific names for a person, place, thing, etc. In these cases, we capitalize the noun, but there are exceptions, for example, if a company decides to have its name in lowercase.

Examples:

  • I work at Platzi.
  • My friend Ariel said, "hello."
  • Her Samsung phone fell and broke.

Plural Nouns

Add -s / -es:

  • I received 3 emails.

Change f to -ves:

  • The shelves were empty.

Change y to -ies:

  • Those were good stories.

Irregular Nouns

Irregular nouns change completely when going from singular to plural.

Examples:

Man:

  • Superman is the best.
  • X-men are the best.

Mouse:

  • They bought a new mouse for me.
  • They bought mice for the new computers.

Person:

  • The person hired will start next week.
  • The people hired will start next week.

Gerunds

Gerunds are another type of noun. They look like a verb plus -**ing**, but they don't refer to an action; they refer to an activity.

Examples:

  • Seeing is not always believing.
  • Listening actively is crucial in a business meeting.
  • Rita enjoys going out with her coworkers.

Countable and Uncountable Nouns

Countable Nouns

Countable nouns are nouns that can be counted.

  • Ryan created two reports.
  • Platzi has 250 employees.
  • I received an interesting task today.

A/An

Use a and an to refer to a singular countable noun.

  • George had a very creative idea.

Uncountable Nouns

Uncountable nouns can't be counted, like water or sugar.

  • Janice gave feedback.
  • I took notes during the meeting.
  • The new position requires a lot of concentration.

Quantifiers

Quantifiers are used with countable and uncountable nouns to indicate amounts or quantities.

Examples:

Some: Use some when referring to a specified amount or quantity in affirmative sentences and questions.

  • They offer some cool benefits.
  • Does he have some news?

Any: Use any when referring to an unspecified amount or quantity in negative sentences and questions.

  • Jen didn’t receive any documents.
  • Did you have any work to do?

A lot of: Use a lot of to refer to a large quantity.

  • We have a lot of projects.
  • Tiana has a lot of experience.

A few / A little: Use a few with plural countable nouns to refer to small quantities.

  • We have a few new teams.

Use a little with uncountable nouns.

  • Henry found a little research.

How many / How much: Use how many with plural countable nouns to ask questions.

  • How many job offers did she receive?

Use how much with uncountable nouns.

  • How much money do you have?

Subject and Object Pronouns and Reflexive Pronouns

To understand the importance of pronouns, let’s use an example:

Olivia is a training manager. Olivia prepares very well for Olivia’s trainings. Olivia records Olivia so Olivia can review what Olivia is going to say.

That sounds awkward, right? We use pronouns to sound less repetitive. Let’s read the paragraph again, but this time with pronouns.

Olivia is a training manager. She prepares very well for her trainings. She records herself so she can review what she is going to say.

So, pronouns are words that can replace nouns.

Indefinite pronouns

Indefinite pronouns are words that we use when we don't know or don't want to specify the amount, people, place, or thing we're talking about. Let's take a closer look.

When Referring to People...

  • Everyone went to the event.
  • Somebody did the research.
  • Nobody joined the meeting.
  • I didn't know anyone at the event.

When Referring to Things or Places…

  • I want to go somewhere.
  • I have something to show you.
  • Nothing happened.
  • We looked everywhere.

Examples

  • Everyone loves Fridays.
  • Something was missing from my desk.
  • Nobody missed the training. I guess they really liked the topic.

Action verbs or linking verbs

Action verbs are verbs that express an action. However, action verbs can be divided into two categories: transitive verbs and intransitive verbs.

Transitive Verbs

Transitive verbs require a direct object to make sense.

Examples:

  • Wendy delivered the project on time.
  • Philip and Jake contacted Tina.

Intransitive Verbs

Intransitive verbs do not need a direct object to make sense; they can stand alone.

Examples:

  • He agrees.
  • The workshop occurred in the morning.
  • Maggie types fast.

Linking Verbs

Linking verbs do not express an action; instead, they create a link between the subject and the complement. In other words, they help us describe the person, place, or thing. Let's explore the most common linking verbs:

To Be

  • Dan and Khloe are managers.
  • The meeting was very long.
  • Amy is a graphic designer.

State or Being

  • The platform seemed easy to use.
  • The new employee became an important asset to the team.

Sensory Verbs

  • That looks great.
  • The construction site sounds noisy due to ongoing renovations.
  • The new office feels spacious and comfortable.

Static Verbs and auxiliary Verbs

Auxiliary Verbs

Auxiliary verbs are also known as helping verbs. They provide us with information about the tense, mood, voice, and other important aspects of the main verb. Let's see the most common ones.

"Be," "Do," "Have"

Frank was correcting a mistake.

I don't have meetings in the mornings.

Has Lyne read the document?

Modal Verbs

  • Could you please print on both sides?
  • Noah might arrive late. (Noah podría llegar tarde)
  • They must finish today.

Stative Verbs

Stative verbs are used to describe a state of being, a feeling, a perception, or a possession. They do not describe actions.

Linking verbs specifically connect the subject to a complement.

Mental or Cognitive State

  • Lia understood what you said.
  • We believe in our team's ability to finish the project early.

Emotional State

  • Ryan likes the new update on the app.
  • I appreciate your effort on this task.

Senses

  • Do you hear her?
  • Do you see any problems with that proposal?
  • This coffee tastes delicious.

Possession

  • I own a small business and manage a team of employees. (Tengo un pequeño negocio y manejo un equipo de empleados)
  • Kevin has excellent problem-solving skills.

Other

  • The report contains detailed financial information.
  • The project involves collaborating with multiple teams. (El proyecto implica colaborar con múltiples equipos)
  • The safety regulations concern all employees.
  • She deserves recognition for her outstanding performance. (Ella merece reconocimiento por su rendimiento excepcional)

Regular Verbs

When we put these regular verbs in the past tense form and the past participle form, we just add ed.

  • Present simple: I always check the report.
  • Past tense: I checked the report yesterday.
  • Past participle: I have just checked the report. (Acabo de comprobar el informe)

Irregular Verbs

When you put these irregular verbs in the past tense form or in the past participle form, they’re either going to change completely, partially, or they’re going to stay the same.

  • Present simple: Tim writes a book every year.
  • Past tense: Tim wrote a book last year.
  • Past participle: Tim has written many books. (Tim ha escrito muchos libros)

Pronunciation of the regular verb in the past

There are three ways to pronounce the ED ending:

  • /d/ Analyzed
  • /t/ Developed
  • /ɪd/ Needed

Voiced Sounds

We’re going to make a /d/ sound when the verb ends in a voiced sound. But what is a voiced sound? It's when we use our vocal cords. Meaning that if the verb ends with some of these letters, it's a voiced sound:

  • b, v, g, z, j, l, m, n, r, or a vowel.

Examples:

  • Mail - Mailed.
  • Borrow - Borrowed.
  • Close - Closed.
  • Encourage - Encouraged.

Unvoiced Sounds

We’re going to make the /t/ sound when the verb ends in an unvoiced sound. An unvoiced sound is when we don’t use our vocal cords. Meaning that if the verb ends with some of these letters, it's an unvoiced sound:

  • p, f, k, s, sh, ch.

Examples:

  • Talk - Talked.
  • Practice - Practiced.
  • Laugh - Laughed. (reír)
  • Approach - Approached.

/t/ or /d/ Sound

We’re going to make an /ɪd/ sound when the verb ends in a /t/ or /d/ sound.

Examples:

  • Vote - Voted.
  • Include - Included.
  • Contact - Contacted.
  • Decide - Decided.

Adjectives

Adjectives are words that describe or modify a noun or a pronoun, and in a sentence with a linking verb, they provide that description.

Examples:

  • The company provides a positive work environment.
  • Dylan is eloquent.

Order to Describe Something

Let’s say we want to describe something using as many adjectives as possible. It's necessary to follow an order to describe that thing we want to talk about.

  1. Quantity

  2. Opinion

  3. Size

  4. Age

  5. Shape

  6. Color

  7. Origin

  8. Material

  9. Type

  10. Purpose

Examples:

  • Lia wrote five (1), strong (2), short (3) paragraphs.
  • She bought a beautiful (1), small (3), square (5), green (6), Japanese (7), organic (8) journal.
  • Jason created a fun (2), colorful (6), sales (9) campaign.
  • The company is hiring two (1), analytical (2), organized (2) professionals.
  • The three (1), long (3) reports were delivered on time.

Comparative Adjectives

These adjectives help us compare one thing with another or one person with another person.

Examples:

  • This app is faster than that one.
  • Henry is more punctual than Mia.
  • Peter is busier than me.

Superlative Adjectives

We use these adjectives when we want to compare one thing with a group of things or one person to a group of people.

Examples:

  • This app is the fastest one.
  • Henry is the most punctual.
  • Peter is the busiest in the office.

Possessive Adjectives vs. Possessive Nouns

Possessive Adjectives

Possessive adjectives are words that indicate who the noun belongs to. Let’s look at these possessive adjectives:

  • I → My
  • She → Her
  • He → His
  • It → Its
  • We → Our
  • They → Their
  • You → Your

Examples:

  • Their computer crashed, so they had to use my laptop for the meeting.
  • That is your cubicle.
  • Josh gave feedback to his team.
  • Our department achieved its quarterly sales targets.
  • Her presentation skills are excellent.

Possessive Pronouns

These words indicate ownership or possession.

  • I → Mine
  • She → Hers
  • He → His
  • It → *
  • We → Ours
  • They → Theirs
  • You → Yours

Examples:

  • I completed my part of the report, and now it’s yours to review.
  • The port is hers, and she is responsible for its successful completion.
  • The decision is his. He has the final say in this matter.
  • The presentation is theirs; they collaborated on it and did an excellent job.

Let’s remember that we use pronouns to sound less repetitive. So instead of saying:

  • Those are my headphones, not your headphones.
  • Those are my headphones, not yours.

Both are correct, but the first one is a bit repetitive. We can combine them to sound more natural.

Adverbs of time and place

Adverbs

Adverbs are going to give us more information about how, when or where an action takes place. They are going to modify or describe verbs.

Verbs:

  • They worked here.

Adjectives:

  • The project outcome was unexpectedly successful.

Adverbs:

  • He finished the project surprisingly quickly.

Adverbs of time

Adverbs of time are going to answer question, such (como) as when.

When?

  • Jill had a one on one session yesterday.
  • I commute to the office in the mornings.

How long?

  • Eric prepared for the conference for 3 days.
  • Allison and Jill have worked on the project all month.

How often?

  • Rick always employs advanced technology in the apps.
  • We frequently attend training session to enhance (mejorar) our skills.

Adverbs of place

  • Michael moved abroad for a new job opportunity.

  • Adverb: The supervisor walked around to ensure everything was in order.
  • Preposition: The supervisor walked around the office to ensure everything was in worder.

Coordinating conjunctions

To understand when to use "Coordinating Conjunctions," let's consider an example:

Without "Coordinating Conjunctions"

I saw the invitation to the meeting in my calendar. I saw the invitation in my email. I can't attend the meeting; I'm going on vacation that same day.

With "Coordinating Conjunctions"

I saw the invitation to the meeting in my calendar and in my email, but I can't attend the meeting, for I'll be on vacation that same day.

Coordinating conjunctions are used to connect words, phrases, and sentences. Let's explore some examples:

For

  • I need to attend the meeting, for I am the project lead.

And

  • Carol and Derek went to the boardroom to present the proposal and discuss the budget.

Nor

  • The project is not on schedule, nor is the budget within the allocated limits.

But

  • The new software has improved efficiency, but it still has some glitches.

Or

  • You can submit the document as a PDF or a Word file.

Yet

  • I have submitted my proposal, yet I haven't received any response from the client.

So

  • We completed the project ahead of schedule, so we decided to take a well-deserved break.

Interjections are words that express emotions or reactions. They're like "Bazinga" or "Dope" from popular shows, such as Sheldon Cooper's "Bazinga" in The Big Bang Theory or Homer's "Dope" in The Simpsons.

TYPES OF INTERJECTIONS

1. EMOTIVE INTERJECTIONS

Emotive interjections convey feelings like happiness, anger, or surprise.

  • Example: “We finished the project on time. Whoop!”
  • Another: “Awww, that's such a beautiful gesture.”
  • Last one: “Ugh, I really don't like long meetings.”

2. COGNITIVE INTERJECTIONS

Cognitive interjections express understanding or an internal reaction to information.

  • Example: “Well, we could implement this feature, but it may require additional resources and time.”
  • Another: “Hmm, I think that's a great idea.”

3. VIOLATIVE INTERJECTIONS

Violative interjections convey commands or warnings.

  • Example: “Let’s go! We’re going to be late.”
  • Another: “Shhh! I can't hear him.”
  • Last one: “Lookout, the competitor just launched a new product.”

4. INTERJECTIONS FOR GREETINGS AND PARTING WORDS

Interjections are also used in greetings and farewells.

  • Example: “Hello, how are you?”
  • Another: “Bye, it was nice seeing you again.”
  • Last one: “Hey team! Ready to tackle today's challenges?”
